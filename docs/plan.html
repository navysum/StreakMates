Habits With Friends

A habit tracker where the streak isn't only yours. Keep your private habits private — and put the ones you keep flaking on in front of four people who will notice.

01

The one idea to get right

Everything else in this document falls out of this single decision.

Most habit trackers give each person their own private list. Yours has two kinds of habit, and the difference is one nullable column:

The core model

A private habit is a row owned by you with no group. A shared habit is one row owned by a group — and every member checks in against that same row. Nothing gets copied per person. A table of check-ins keyed by (habit, person, date) does all the work.

That's why "The Gym Rats go to the gym 4× a week" can be one thing you all look at, rather than five separate habits that happen to share a name. When Maya taps her circle, she inserts a row for herself against the group's habit. The group board is just: for today's date, who has a row and who doesn't.

Once you see check-ins as facts about a person on a day rather than as state stored on the habit, the streak maths, the group board, the activity feed and the offline behaviour all get easy.

04

How the data is shaped

Seven tables. If you understand these, you understand the app.

profiles

  • id uuid → auth user
  • display_name text
  • avatar_url text
  • timezone text
Created automatically the first time someone signs in with Google.

groups

  • id uuid
  • name text
  • emoji text
  • invite_code text · unique
  • created_by uuid
The invite code lives here and can be rotated by the owner.

group_members

  • group_id uuid
  • user_id uuid
  • role owner | member
  • joined_at timestamptz
Key is the pair, so nobody joins twice. This table decides who sees what.

habits

  • id uuid
  • owner_id uuid
  • group_id uuid · nullable
  • title, emoji, colour
  • cadence daily | weekly | days
  • target_days int[]
  • reminder_at time
  • archived_at timestamptz
group_id null = private. group_id set = shared. That column is the whole feature.

check_ins

  • id uuid
  • habit_id uuid
  • user_id uuid
  • local_date date
  • note text
  • created_at timestamptz
Unique on (habit, user, date). One tap per person per day, and safe to retry.

reactions

  • check_in_id uuid
  • user_id uuid
  • emoji text
🔥 and 👏 on a friend's check-in. One of each per person.

nudges

  • habit_id uuid
  • from_user / to_user uuid
  • created_at timestamptz
Rate-limit to one per person per habit per day or friendships will suffer.

Streaks are calculated, not stored

Resist the urge to keep a streak number on the habit. A stored counter drifts the moment a check-in arrives late from an offline phone. Read the last N check-in dates and count backwards — it's fast for years of data, and it is always correct. Denormalise later only if a real screen gets slow.

05

How the invite code works

Six characters, and one small piece of server-side care that stops them being guessable at scale.

  1. Someone creates a group. The database generates a six-character code from an alphabet with no 0, O, 1 or I in it — about a billion combinations, none of which get misread over a pub table.
  2. They share it. The phone's normal share sheet sends “Join The Gym Rats on Habits: 7KQ2FM”, plus a deep link that pre-fills the code if the app is installed.
  3. A friend types the code. The app doesn't search the groups table — it calls one database function, join_group_with_code.
  4. That function runs with elevated rights and does three things: find the group, insert the membership, return the group. Because it's the only door in, ordinary users never get read access to the full groups table, so nobody can scrape codes.
  5. They're in. Every shared habit in that group appears in their Shared tab immediately, and the whole group sees “Sam joined” in Activity.
Guard rail

Rate-limit that function per user — something like ten attempts an hour. Without it, a billion combinations is only a few days of brute force for a determined stranger. With it, it's forever.

06

Who can see what

The part most people get wrong, and the part that matters most when the app holds “No phone after 10pm”.

Postgres can enforce visibility rules in the database itself, so the phone app is never trusted to filter anything. Even someone poking at the API directly gets back only their own rows. The rules read almost like English:

TableWho can readWho can write
profilesYou, plus anyone sharing a group with youOnly your own
groupsMembers onlyThe owner
group_membersMembers of that groupYourself, to leave; the owner, to remove
habitsThe owner, or members of its groupThe owner; group habits, any member
check_insAnyone who can see the habitOnly rows where the user is you
reactions / nudgesGroup membersOnly as yourself
The trap everybody hits

Writing “you can read group_members if you're in group_members” makes Postgres check the rule to check the rule, forever, and every query dies with an infinite-recursion error. Fix it before you write a single policy: put the membership check in a small helper function marked security definer, and call that from every policy instead. One function, no recursion, and the rules stay one line each.

Private means private. A private habit has no group, and no policy anywhere grants another human read access to it. Make the private/shared choice explicit when a habit is created, and warn clearly before making an existing private habit shared — that action can't be un-seen.

07

Five things that will bite you

Every one of these is cheap to handle now and expensive to retrofit.

  • “Today” is not a server concept. Someone checking in at 11pm in Sydney and someone at 8am in London must land on their own dates. Work out the date on the phone from the user's timezone, send it as local_date, and never let the server derive the day from a timestamp.
  • The App Store will reject Google-only login. If you offer a third-party sign-in, Apple requires an equally private alternative. Sign in with Apple satisfies it and takes an afternoon. Build it before your first submission, not after your first rejection.
  • Taps happen underground and on planes. Write the check-in to local storage first, show the tick immediately, and queue the upload. The unique key on (habit, user, date) means a retry that arrives twice is harmless — the second one just does nothing.
  • Nudges become harassment fast. One nudge per person, per habit, per day, and a switch in settings to turn them off entirely. Social pressure is the feature; being pestered is how the app gets deleted.
  • Leaving a group is a data question. Decide now: does Sam's history vanish from the board when he leaves, or stay as a record? Keeping the rows but hiding the person is usually right, and it's much harder to add once people have left.

Build order

Each phase ends with something you can open on your own phone. Never build two of these at once.

PHASE 0SkeletonExpo app running on your phone, Supabase project created, tab navigation with four empty screens.Ends with: an app icon on your home screen.
PHASE 1Login and private habitsGoogle sign-in, profile row created on first launch, create a habit, tap to check in, streak counted. No sharing at all yet.Ends with: a habit tracker you'd actually use alone.
PHASE 2Groups and codesCreate a group, generate the code, join by code, member list. Permission rules written and tested with two real accounts.Ends with: your friend's face in your app.
PHASE 3Shared habitsGroup habits, the Shared tab, and the group board grid. Live updates so a friend's tick appears without a refresh.Ends with: the actual product.
PHASE 4The social layerActivity feed, emoji reactions, nudges with their rate limit. This is what turns a tool into something people open daily.Ends with: a reason to come back.
PHASE 5Reminders and polishPush notifications, offline queue, empty states, the heatmap, editing and archiving habits, account deletion.Ends with: something you can hand to a stranger.
PHASE 6Ship itSign in with Apple, privacy policy, store screenshots, App Store and Play Store listings, TestFlight round with the group you built it for.Ends with: a link you can text people.

Phases 0–3 are the real build; everything after is finishing. If you only get one thing from this plan, take the shape of check_ins — one row per person, per habit, per day — and the rest follows.
